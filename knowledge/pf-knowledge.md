### Nov/Dec 2026: TPNW topic primer

**What the TPNW is**
- Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons ("ban treaty"). Adopted 7 July 2017 at the UN (122 votes), opened for signature 20 Sept 2017, entered into force 22 Jan 2021 after the 50th ratification.
- **Article 1** bans developing, testing, producing, acquiring, possessing, stockpiling, using or *threatening to use* nuclear weapons, and *assisting, encouraging or inducing* anyone to do so.
  - **1(1)(e)/(f)**: no assisting or encouraging. This is the core clash with NATO's nuclear planning (the Nuclear Planning Group) and with deterrence doctrine.
  - **1(1)(g)**: no "stationing, installation or deployment" of nuclear weapons on a state's territory. This directly conflicts with **NATO nuclear sharing**: US B61 bombs are hosted in **Belgium, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands** and Türkiye (Türkiye being "European" is debatable), and their dual-capable aircraft (F-35A, Tornado) are part of the mission.
- **Article 4** gives nuclear-armed states two pathways: "destroy then join" or "join then destroy" under a time-bound plan. This matters because **France and the UK are European NATO members with their own arsenals**. Affirming means they would have to disarm.
- **Articles 6 and 7** set positive obligations: assistance for victims and environmental remediation.
- **Article 3** safeguards: at minimum the existing IAEA safeguards, with no requirement for the Additional Protocol. This is a frequent con "weak verification" argument.
- **Article 17**: withdrawal on 12 months' notice (*not* during an armed conflict the state is party to).
- **Article 18**: the treaty doesn't prejudice other obligations "consistent with" it. Con teams argue this means the TPNW overrides NATO commitments.
- Meetings of States Parties: 1MSP Vienna 2022 (Germany, Norway, Netherlands, Belgium and Finland attended as observers), 2MSP New York 2023, 3MSP 2025. A first Review Conference is expected around 2026. Check current details.
- No NATO member has signed. In Europe, Austria, Ireland and Malta are parties, but they're EU members, not NATO members.
- NATO's official line (North Atlantic Council, 2017/2020) is that it opposes the treaty because it "does not reflect the increasingly challenging international security environment."

**Pro (Aff) argument families**
- Humanitarian consequences and risk: accident, miscalculation, and escalation in the Russia–NATO context (Ukraine, tactical nukes in Belarus, Kaliningrad Iskanders).
- Norm-building and stigmatization: the treaty changes the legitimacy of nukes, as with chemical weapons and landmines (Ottawa Treaty precedent).
- Fulfilling **NPT Article VI** disarmament obligations and saving the NPT's credibility, in the run-up to and fallout from the NPT RevCon.
- Host nations are targets: removing B61s reduces first-strike targeting.
- Democratic legitimacy: polls (e.g., ICAN/YouGov) show majorities in Germany, Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands favor joining.
- European strategic autonomy: decoupling from an unreliable US nuclear guarantee.
- Divestment and financial stigma, plus victim assistance and remediation.

**Con (Neg) argument families**
- Extended deterrence and **Article 5** credibility collapse, which emboldens Russian aggression (Baltics, Suwalki gap).
- Alliance cohesion: a split NATO, US abandonment, and decoupling.
- **Allied proliferation**: if the umbrella goes, Poland, Germany or Türkiye may build their own weapons, or the Gulf and Asia proliferate in cascade.
- Unilateral disarmament: Russia and China aren't bound, so the deterrence balance shifts.
- The TPNW undermines the NPT and IAEA verification, with no Additional Protocol required.
- The France/UK deterrent and the "Eurodeterrent" debates (Macron's offer to discuss extending French deterrence) are incompatible with the treaty.
- Spending and conventional arms races: NATO's 5%-of-GDP target (2025 Hague summit) and a costlier conventional substitute for nukes.
- Practical problems: B61-12 modernization, F-35 purchases already committed, and withdrawal clauses.

**Definitions to look out for**
- "European members of NATO": collectively (all of them, including France and the UK) or individually? Is Türkiye included?
- "Ratify": domestic approval of the treaty plus deposit of the instrument. Signing comes first, and none have signed.
- Extended deterrence, nuclear umbrella, nuclear sharing, dual-capable aircraft (DCA), strategic stability, the escalate-to-de-escalate doctrine, and the ban treaty versus a step-by-step approach.

## 2. Public Forum jargon (the pet should talk like this)

- **Card**: a piece of evidence: a **tag** (claim), a **cite** (author, date and credentials) and the **body** (verbatim text, underlined and highlighted).
- **Cut a card**: copy original text verbatim, then underline and highlight the words you'll read aloud. Unread text stays in small font and is never deleted.
- **Tag / power tag**: a one-sentence argumentative claim the card proves. Don't overclaim.
- **Highlighting**: the words actually read in round. Read alone, it must make grammatical sense.
- **Emphasis (bold)**: the key words to stress.
- **Card clipping**: claiming to read text you skipped. This is an evidence ethics violation.
- **Paraphrasing**: summarizing instead of reading the text. The NSDA restricts it heavily, and it must match the original.
- **Brackets**: changing words is not allowed if it alters meaning. Never add words.
- **Case / contention / subpoint**: the prepared constructive speech.
- **Uniqueness**: the status quo, or why the impact happens only in one world.
- **Link / internal link / impact**: the causal chain.
- **Warrant**: the reason *why* a claim is true. Good cards have warrants, not just claims.
- **Turn**: link turn (your link actually goes the other way) or impact turn (the impact is good). Never run both on the same argument, because that's a double turn.
- **Delink / no link, mitigation, non-unique (NU)**: defense.
- **Frontline / block**: prepared responses to an argument.
- **Weighing**: magnitude, probability, scope, timeframe, reversibility, strength of link, clarity, prerequisite, short-circuit. A meta-weigh compares the weighing mechanisms themselves.
- **Extension**: carrying an argument through later speeches. Summary and final focus must extend link + impact + warrant.
- **Collapse**: going for one or two arguments in the back half.
- **Speeches**: Constructive (4 min), Rebuttal (4), Summary (3), Final Focus (2), Crossfire (3), Grand Crossfire (3), plus 3 minutes of prep per team.
- **Squo**: status quo. **Aff/Neg**: Pro/Con. **Prep**: prep time. **Doc**: a case document. **Speech doc / evidence exchange / "can I see that card?"**: the opposing team's right to request evidence.
- **Progressive PF**: theory (disclosure, paraphrase theory), kritiks (Ks), and frameworks.

## 3. Evidence rules (NSDA-based; follow strictly)

- The author's name and the date must be read aloud. The full citation must be available on request: the author's **full name**, **qualifications**, **publication date**, **source/publication**, **article title**, **full URL**, and the **date accessed** for online evidence. Missing evidence counts as nonexistent.
- Card text must be **verbatim** from the original. Never alter words, insert words, or change meaning by omitting context.
- Unread text in the middle of a passage stays in the card (small font). Never use ellipses to cut text out of a passage.
- Distorting evidence (claiming something it doesn't say) and fabricating evidence are disqualifying offenses.
- Prefer recent, qualified, and reputable sources: think tanks (CSIS, Carnegie, RAND, Chatham House, SIPRI, ELN, IISS, Brookings, Arms Control Association, BASIC), academic journals, major newspapers, and government and NATO docs.
- Avoid Wikipedia, anonymous blogs, AI-generated content, and other debaters' briefs as primary sources.
